The Forgotten Ones

The flagship keynote

The students who never raise their hand, never cause trouble, and never get called on are often the ones most at risk. This keynote challenges educators to build systems of visibility before they build systems of achievement. The forgotten ones are not failing. They are waiting to be found.

Best for Educators, school leaders, counselors, and parents

Pause. Pivot. Point.

A framework for reaching the students your school almost lost

Hakeem Olajuwon didn't invent a move. He invented a moment. The Dream Shake becomes a framework for the hardest minutes of the school day: the reaction you avoid, the different move you make, and the lesson underneath. It is a move, not a mood, and everyone in the room leaves with their own version written on paper.

Stop Appeasing. Start Leading.

Appeasement feels like peace. It is surrender. This keynote separates the deal from the standard, kindness from capitulation, and de-escalation from weakness, then shows leaders how to build structure that makes the right move the default move.

The motto behind Shane's work is a sequence, not a slogan. A person has to believe they matter before they can see a purpose, and see a purpose before success becomes believable. This keynote shows how a school, a home, or a team builds that belief one small win at a time.

Two moments from the job.

The first is about the pause. The second is about the point.

The axe

I was an assistant principal at a high school. I walk into the cafeteria a few minutes before lunch, before six or seven hundred kids come through those doors, and sitting on the window sill is an axe.

Not a hatchet. A full-size chopping axe.

There is no other adult in the room. I can't leave to go secure it, because in about four minutes this room fills up.

So I picked it up, threw it over my shoulder, and walked around the cafeteria like I normally would.

I got some strange looks. A comment or two. Mostly, kids didn't think much about it at all.

I've thought about why for years. It's not that I handled it brilliantly. It's that those kids already knew me. I was the guy checking out their books. I was the guy at their games hollering for them. So when they saw me walking the room with an axe on my shoulder, the read wasn't threat. The read was Mr. Nelson is handling something.

The pause itself takes half a second. The credit you spend in that half second took two years to earn.

The cafeteria

Every year at graduation I say something about the students who aren't walking. In 2026 there were two. Not finished. Not forgotten. Just not done yet.

One of them was three classes shy when our deadline hit. He had struggled in the past, but on this day he was looking healthy and sharp. I stood next to him and told him we'd work all summer. That we'd get him there. He was sad, but he understood.

Later that same day I was in the cafeteria and I saw him sitting at a table. I walked over. He looked up at me and said:

“Mr. Nelson, looks like you've lost a lot of weight.”

I said yeah, over forty pounds. I told him I spend so much time up here working with you all that sometimes I forget to take care of myself. And I decided it was time.

He looked at me and said:

“Me too. I'm taking care of myself too.”

A kid who found out that morning he wasn't going to walk with his class, telling his principal a few hours later that he's taking care of himself too. Not giving up. Not checked out.

And notice what I did not do in that cafeteria. I did not turn it into a lesson. It was already the moment, and narrating it would have made it mine instead of his.

Sometimes the Point is something you name out loud. Sometimes it is something you receive and protect and don't touch.

What we built at Putnam Heights.

None of it was required. All of it was intentional.

Nobody gave us a facility. We got one summer a year, and we used it. When the district's facility money came through and the default suggestion was to renovate the cafeteria, I said no. I sat down with a local architect and we designed a library from scratch: the lighting, esports built into it, STEM resources, an environment so visually compelling that students couldn't help but want to be in it.

Shane Nelson is a public education veteran who has served as a teacher, assistant principal, principal, and founding school leader across urban, suburban, rural, regular, and alternative settings. He is Director of Alternative Education for Oklahoma City Public Schools, where he oversees three schools and a range of programs serving more than a thousand students. He is the founding principal of Putnam Heights Academy, a campus built for students facing high levels of trauma and behavioral challenge.

He created a districtwide esports program, was named a Principal of the Year honoree, and has designed student-centered spaces on more than a dozen campuses. He started college as a civil engineering major, drawn to how things are built. A summer at the YMCA showed him that his first love was working with people.
